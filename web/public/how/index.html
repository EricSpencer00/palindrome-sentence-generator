How the palindrome paragraph generator works

This page explains how the generator builds paragraphs that read the same backwards, how their sentences are scored, and how the output compares with earlier versions and with real English. The short version: it writes grammatical sentences, and it still leans too hard on two-letter words.

Definition of a palindrome paragraph

A palindrome paragraph is a paragraph whose letters read the same in both directions once spaces, punctuation and capitals are removed. Step on no pets becomes steponnopets, which reverses to itself. Only the letters have to match. Words, spaces and punctuation don’t, so No, will I hate me here? on the left is answered by Ere he met a hill. I won. on the right, although no word on one side matches a word on the other. That freedom is what makes long palindromes possible, and it is also why most long palindromes read badly: letters can be cut into words almost any way at all.

How the generator pays letter debts

The generator treats a palindrome as a ledger. It writes from the middle out. Each word added to one half creates a debt: the other half owes those letters, reversed. Whichever half owes adds words to pay. A word that overpays turns its surplus into a debt for the other side. The paragraph is finished when nothing is owed.

Why most valid moves produce gibberish

Each debt can be paid in many ways, and most of them are nonsense. Hate me here reverses to ereh em etah. Cut differently, the same letters give ere he met ah, and with a little more, ere he met a hill. A search that only cares about matching letters can’t tell these apart, and it quickly learns that tiny words are the cheapest payment: a, I, no, it, on. Keeping that habit in check is the main job of the scoring described below.

Beam search: hundreds of drafts at once

A depth-first search commits to one path and dies in its first dead end. The generator runs a beam search: it holds a few hundred half-built paragraphs at once, extends every one of them by every legal word, scores the results and keeps only the best few hundred for the next round. A debt is only allowed if some sequence of dictionary words can still pay it, so the beam never wastes a slot on a hopeless paragraph.

How each sentence is scored

Two scorers decide what "best" means. The first is a trigram language model trained on 3.8 million words of the Brown corpus and Project Gutenberg. It rewards sequences English actually uses. Commas, full stops and question marks are tokens to it but cost no letters, so the search can end a sentence wherever the grammar wants a break.

The second is a counting verifier that judges each sentence the moment its full stop lands. It uses no neural network: it counts part-of-speech patterns from the Brown corpus and the share of filler words, then penalises repeated content words and non-words. The result is a probability that the sentence is readable English, fitted to separate human palindromes from machine word salad. Sentences that fall below the line drag their whole paragraph down the beam. A counting model is the better tool here than a neural judge: it runs in a fraction of a millisecond, so it can score every candidate instead of only the finalists, and every penalty it gives can be traced to a count.

What the results don’t prove

The readability score flatters the generator. The search was optimised against it, so two thirds of sentences rated readable, level with real English, says little on its own. GPT-2 is a fairer judge because the search never consults it, and it puts the generator at a median perplexity of 205: more than six times better than v1 at 1,273, and still about four times worse than ordinary English at 55. v1’s row is thinner than it looks, because 50 runs gave only 6 distinct paragraphs.

Short words are the weakest number. 45% of the generator’s words have one or two letters, against 30% for v1 and 21% for English. The grammar is paid for with a, I, no and it, and fixing that is the most important open problem. The sentences also lean on familiar phrasing, because nothing in the search penalises it yet. No blinded human reader study has been run on this version, and none of these numbers replace one.

Prior work: Hoey and Norvig

In 1984 Dan Hoey wrote a program that extended A man, a plan, a canal: Panama to hundreds of words by tracking the letters one side owed the other. In 2002 Peter Norvig rebuilt the idea as a search and documented it. Their palindromes are impressive and unreadable: exact, enormous lists of names and nouns. This generator keeps their bookkeeping and aims for prose instead of length.

Versions 1 to 4 and why they failed

  1. v1 searched word by word, scored by a bigram model and reranked by GPT-2. Exact at any length, word salad at every length. A blinded judge rejected all 25 of its sentences.
  2. v2 placed about 1,300 whole sentences mined from Wikipedia into the mirror. It read better because it quoted instead of writing.
  3. v3 nested 540 verified palindromes from an offline bank. It stalled near a thousand letters, and readers preferred the shorter original over the grown paragraph in 20 of 20 pairs.
  4. v4 searched grammatical paths while matching letters at the seams. Its best reached 650 letters and still repeated names and broke clauses.

Two conclusions came out of these failures. Readability has to be scored during the search; checking it at the end is too late, because by then every candidate is already gibberish. And banning bad phrases doesn’t work: in a controlled study of 360 matched runs, banning the six most reused phrases removed them and raised lexical diversity, and the search settled into new repeated phrases instead.
