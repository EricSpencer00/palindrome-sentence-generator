\begin{abstract}
Exact letter-level palindromes preserve a global character constraint while still needing to read as English. We report a paired-edit construction in which mirrored insertions obey a local string equation, and audit a saved lineage from 568 to 752 letters. A separate bounded search records one exact 672-letter construction. We also compare nine selected project outputs from 498 to 752 letters with held-out Brown prose spans matched by alphabetic-token count. Every prose control has higher Brown word-bigram order gain (mean 1.462 vs. 0.256 nats per transition). Across 108 additional intact controls from 16 to about 2,050 tokens, every control scores above its own word-shuffled versions. This statistic measures local word order, not readability: it does not model syntax, meaning, or discourse, and no human ratings were collected. We provide the exactness checks and recorded diagnostic in an auditable case study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A letter-level palindrome reads the same after its letters are reversed, ignoring case, spaces, and punctuation. The short example \emph{An aide rips nine memos; some men inspire Diana.} has 38 letters under this definition. It is an inherited reference, not a result produced by the constructions studied here. Extending an exact tape is straightforward if words do not matter; finding longer text that also reads naturally is harder because word boundaries and sentence structure must fit the character constraint.

Prior work has generated palindromes by searching n-gram corpora and by working from unmatched remainders \citep{papadopoulos2015palindromes,norvig2002palindrome}. Constraint-based language generation offers related ways to compose text under lexical and structural restrictions \citep{bonlarron-regin-2024}. We study a simple complementary construction: edit two locations in an existing exact tape, preserving the intervening text and solving for the mirrored character sequence. Our aim is to document this construction and its evidence, not to make a general claim about palindrome search or readable long-form generation.

The paper makes two measurements that are often conflated explicit. First, exactness and length can be checked mechanically. Second, prose quality needs its own evidence. We report a 568-to-752-letter saved lineage and a separate 672-letter search result. To assess one narrow aspect of language, we compare selected outputs with intact Brown-corpus spans using a transparent word-bigram diagnostic. That diagnostic is deliberately not called a readability score: it only tests whether a text's local word order is preferred to shuffles of the same words.

\section{Paired edits}

For rendered text $x$, let $\norm{x}$ lowercase and keep only ASCII letters. An exact palindrome satisfies $\norm{x}=\rev(\norm{x})$. Work below is on normalized letter strings. Write an exact parent as
\begin{equation}
 p=AqC\rev(q)\rev(A), \qquad C=\rev(C),
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the unchanged bridge between the edit sites and $C$ is the center. Insert equal-length strings $U$ and $V$ on opposite sides of the center:
\begin{equation}
 p'=AUqCV\rev(q)\rev(A).
\end{equation}
The edited tape is exact if and only if
\begin{equation}
 Uq=q\rev(V). \label{eq:seam}
\end{equation}
After the mirrored outer string $A$ is set aside, this equality matches the two strings adjacent to $C$; reversing it matches the corresponding suffixes. Equation~\ref{eq:seam} describes the first insertion in the 568-to-640 step. The three later lineage edits replace paired spans, so we validate each complete output directly rather than applying the insertion result beyond its assumptions.

\section{Local word-order calibration}

We selected nine project outputs from 498 to 752 letters, including related stages from the 568-to-752 lineage and the separate 672-letter search. A tenth project output of 54 letters and an inherited 38-letter reference are retained in the full diagnostic record but excluded from this long-output comparison. Selection is retrospective and non-exhaustive; several outputs share text and construction history.

The diagnostic uses a word-bigram model trained on 401 Brown documents; 99 different Brown documents are held out for controls. Brown is a collection of edited American English text, accessed through NLTK \citep{kucera-francis-1967,loper-bird-2002-nltk}. We lowercase alphabetic tokens and score each observed adjacent-token sequence with additive smoothing. For each text, \emph{order gain} is its mean bigram log probability minus the mean for 32 deterministic shuffles of the same tokens, in nats per transition. A positive value means the observed order fits this model better than its own shuffles. It does not mean the text is grammatical or understandable.

Each output is paired with a contiguous, intact span from the held-out documents containing the same number of scorer tokens. An additional 108 non-overlapping intact spans calibrate the score at nine target lengths: 16, 32, 64, 128, 192, 256, 512, 1,024, and 2,048 tokens, with 12 spans per target. The longest spans contain 2,042 to 2,050 tokens. All controls were selected before scoring and compared with 32 shuffles of their own words.

Across the nine long outputs, mean order gain is 0.256 nats per transition, compared with 1.462 for the matched prose; every one of the nine prose controls scores higher. The 108 length controls all have positive order gain, with mean values between 1.508 and 1.855 across the length bands. Thus the diagnostic continues to distinguish intact corpus order from shuffled order well beyond the lengths of the palindromes. It does not show that any palindrome is readable, or that longer palindromes are better. The highest-scoring output is the rough 498-letter overhang example, illustrating that a local bigram preference cannot stand in for a human judgment of prose.

\section{Discussion}

The paired-edit condition gives a direct way to preserve exactness while changing content on both sides of an existing tape. In the saved lineage, this supports growth from 568 to 752 letters; a separate construction reaches 672. These are concrete, audited outputs, not a claim that the method finds the longest palindrome or produces consistently fluent text.

The Brown comparison adds a length-matched point of reference for one local language signal. Its gap between selected outputs and intact prose shows why character count and exactness should not be used as substitutes for language evaluation. At the same time, the score sees only adjacent words, and its corpus reflects one variety and period of written American English. Human judgments of readability, coherence, and meaning remain necessary; standard evaluation work recommends explicit criteria and reproducible protocols for such judgments \citep{howcroft-etal-2020-twenty,khashabi-etal-2022-genie}. No reader study was conducted here, and we make no human-readability claim.
