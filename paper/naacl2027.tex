\begin{abstract}
Long English palindromes couple a global character constraint with local word
boundaries and discourse decisions. We study an incremental construction
method that edits a verified palindrome while preserving the text outside
explicitly chosen seams. Each edit tracks the character obligations induced
by retained text, their owning words or clauses, and the positions at which
the two sides must agree. We formalize the seam equation and distinguish authored edit proposals
from a bounded search over typed clauses. A retrospective audit of eleven
saved examples verifies exact letter symmetry independently of the generators.
The examples include a 568-letter parent, a 630-letter child produced at an
asymmetric seam, a 672-letter child found by clause search, and an editing
sequence ending at 752 letters with authored left paths and grammar-parsed
right paths. We provide the rendered passages,
parent links, hashes, and executable checks, and measure repetition separately
from length. These are exact construction results: inherited fragments,
repeated syntax, and unresolved discourse remain visible, and no blinded human
readability result is available. The method supports auditable growth and
local revision of long palindromes; establishing that this growth produces
readable English remains an empirical question.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An English letter-level palindrome must support two simultaneous readings:
its words must form a passage, while its letters must agree with their own
reversal. A change as small as replacing a verb can invalidate the whole
string. Conversely, an exactly symmetric string may consist of familiar
words without conveying a coherent scene. Length, exactness, and readability
therefore describe different properties of a construction.

Our objective is to grow a long passage that a reader can understand. We
investigate a practical intermediate capability: modifying a long verified
palindrome without discarding its existing construction. The central object
is an \emph{editable seam}, a pair of positions whose character obligations
are tracked together. A seam may fall at a sentence boundary, between words,
or within a word. Retaining the surrounding text reduces an edit to a local
equation; the new wording still has to satisfy that equation in context.

Consider two insertions in a 568-letter parent. The left insertion emits
\path{noranorasawgodnadiasawgoddogwas}; the right emits
\path{aronsawgoddogwasaidandogwasaron}. They close after accounting for
the retained four-letter bridge \code{nora}.
The resulting passage has 630 letters and passes an independent full-string
check. Its observation and identity clauses remain awkward. This example
shows both the usefulness and the scope of the method: it makes an exact
edit possible, but does not establish what a reader will understand.

We report three concrete contributions. First, we give a precise account of
residual-constrained editing, including character positions, retained text,
and the distinction between a spelling obligation and a grammatical
obligation. Second, we reproduce long saved constructions and a bounded
clause-search result, explicitly separating automated search from authored
lexical choices. Third, we provide a compact audit in which every displayed
result is tied to its rendered text and source artifact. The largest among
these selected examples has 752 letters; it is a construction endpoint, not a length record
or a human-validated English passage.

This is an incremental editing study. Remainder-based palindrome construction
and joint word--character search predate this work
\citep{norvig2002palindrome,papadopoulos2015palindromes}. Our contribution is
the editing interface, its correctness contract, and its reproducible
application to saved long texts. The equation specializes an existing
remainder-based construction principle to fixed editing positions.

\section{Task and evidence}
\label{sec:task}

For rendered text $x$, $\norm{x}$ retains only the ASCII letters
\code{A--Z} and \code{a--z}, then lowercases them. In particular, it does not
transliterate non-ASCII characters. Let $\rev(s)$ reverse a string $s$.
We require a nonempty tape and
\begin{equation}
  \norm{x}=\rev(\norm{x}).
\end{equation}
All lengths in this paper count normalized letters. Spaces, apostrophes,
and punctuation remain visible in the rendering but contribute no letters.
We preserve the saved wording when displaying an example; punctuation is
never used to hide a character edit.

\paragraph{Mechanical and linguistic outcomes.}
We treat an exact construction as an established mechanical result even when
it contains repeated phrases or poor syntax. Those properties remain
documented targets for revision. A stronger claim of readable English would
require evidence that people can follow the wording and recover its events.
Automatic scores and grammar templates can diagnose particular language
properties; we reserve the readability claim for reader evidence.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
Each audited row identifies a saved source object, its complete rendering,
and a digest of the normalized tape. Parent links distinguish inherited text
from local additions. A check that a proposed phrase is absent from an earlier
repository snapshot establishes only archive-relative novelty. It cannot
establish that the phrase was never previously written, or that every
inherited motif is original. Familiar palindrome motifs and reversible word
pairs occur in the long examples. We make no claim that these passages are
free of such building blocks.

\paragraph{Scope of the snapshot.}
The evaluation is a retrospective selection of eleven saved examples from a
development archive. Selection covers an inherited short reference, a
54-letter noun-phrase candidate over an inherited open frame, a 498-letter
overhang-search output included as contextual evidence, the 568-letter parent, two
asymmetric-seam children, the searched 672-letter child, and the
640--752-letter editing sequence. It is a case study of these mechanisms,
not a random sample or a census of all attempted methods. File-name date
suffixes are identifiers; the audit pins file contents rather than using
those suffixes to infer chronology. We do not aggregate unrelated search
counters into a success rate.

\section{Residual-constrained seam editing}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Representation}

The editor maintains the rendered parent $x$, its normalized tape $p$, and a
mapping from each tape position to the corresponding character in $x$.
An edit record stores two intervals or insertion positions, the proposed
wording, and a residual. An owner identifies the word or clause to which a
character belongs; grammar annotations can record a missing argument,
unresolved attachment, or incomplete word. In the authored experiments these
annotations are supplied with the edit. They are not the output of a general
English parser or semantic verifier.

The separation matters at a partial-word seam. If a retained substring begins
inside a word, its first characters are already committed. A new proposal
must complete that word without duplicating or deleting them. Punctuation
and whitespace must then be rendered at the corresponding raw positions.
Earlier recipes explicitly retain boundaries such as \code{m|e},
\code{op|en}, and \code{i|ts}; their validity comes from the complete
character equation, not from treating either fragment as an English word.

\subsection{An asymmetric seam equation}
\label{sec:equation}

Write a normalized parent as
\begin{equation}
 p=AqC\rev(q)\rev(A),\qquad C=\rev(C),
\end{equation}
where $q$ is a retained \emph{seam bridge}, already mirrored in the parent.
It is not a mismatch in that parent. The offset between editing positions
makes it part of the new spelling obligation. Insert $U$ immediately after $A$ and
$V$ immediately after $C$. The proposed tape is
\begin{equation}
 p'=AUqCV\rev(q)\rev(A).
\end{equation}
The local closure condition is
\begin{equation}
  Uq=q\rev(V).
  \label{eq:seam}
\end{equation}
For equal-length insertions $|U|=|V|$, this condition is necessary and
sufficient for $p'$ to be palindromic.

\paragraph{Proof.}
The outer $A$ and $\rev(A)$ cancel in the forward/reverse comparison.
The two sides surrounding $C$ have equal length. They agree exactly when
$Uq=\rev(V\rev(q))=q\rev(V)$. The remaining center $C$ is already a
palindrome. The argument is reversible. $\square$

The equal-length condition is essential for the stated equivalence: with
unequal insertions, the center may move, so this equation remains sufficient
but need not be necessary. The formula also includes an ordinary symmetric
insertion when $q$ is empty. This theorem concerns insertion into the stated
parent decomposition. The later replacement recipes remove old phrase pairs
and check their new supports and the complete resulting tape. Their saved
outputs are validated independently; we do not apply the insertion theorem
to a replacement without first establishing its required decomposition.

\subsection{Construction procedure}

The implemented recipes follow the same checking protocol, although they
are separate programs rather than one shared search engine:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Load a named parent and check its expected tape digest and exactness.
\item Select the editable positions and record their owning words or clauses.
\item Propose left and right text, retaining the residual required by the
      frozen text. Proposals may be authored or searched; this choice is
      recorded per result.
\item Consume matching characters, retain any unresolved suffix and its
      owner, and reject a conflicting assignment at its first position.
\item Render the splice and check the whole passage independently. Save the
      rendering, new and retained spans, validation, and remaining language
      problems together.
\end{enumerate}

Proposal generation has three forms. The 630-letter recipe supplies both
insertions directly. In the 640--752 sequence, the left event path is authored
and a small role grammar segments its reversed tape into the right event
path. Each of these saved runs yields one right parse before and after its
linkage filter. Thus the right surface is computed, but the system does not
search for the left events. Section~\ref{sec:search} describes a third form
that searches left clauses and their compatible right counterparts.

Replaying a fixed recipe establishes its reproducibility, not a discovery
rate. When an edit fails, its first conflicting positions identify which
wording or seam must change. In each case, exactness is decided on the
complete rendered text after the edit.

\section{A bounded typed-clause search}
\label{sec:search}

We analyze one saved clause-search operator in detail. It starts with
the same 568-letter parent and inserts two blocks at normalized positions
48 and 520, both complete sentence boundaries. Its grammar has ten named
entities and three present-tense transitive predicates:
\emph{sees}, \emph{stops}, and \emph{spots}. The entity inventory is Nora,
Leon, Aron, Noel, Mara, Aram, Nadia, Aidan, Liam, and Ira. Each clause is
subject--predicate--object, giving 300 possible clauses before filtering.
The inventory intentionally contains reverse-compatible spellings. A
fresh exhaustive census removes 30 self-relations and eight palindromic
clauses, leaving 262 clauses and 160 ordered reverse-compatible pairs.
These are clause-level counts before chain and archive filters.

A depth-first search builds four left clauses. After the first clause,
each subject must equal the preceding object. For each proposed clause, the
right grammar begins with the compatible subject--predicate--object options
and removes options one character at a time unless their reversed spelling
matches the emitted left character. The right block is discovered from its
last clause toward its first. An object constraint makes its consecutive
clauses satisfy the same entity-continuity rule in reading order.

At depth four, the operator requires distinct relation triples across both
blocks, at least two predicate spellings on the left, and absence of the
relation strings in a recorded archive index. It excludes self-relations,
repeated clauses, and individually palindromic clauses during construction.
The residual is reset at each completed clause. Thus this experiment tests
online \emph{within-clause} character matching and cross-clause entity
continuity; it does not test character obligations that cross clause
boundaries. Subject--object continuity is a structural constraint, not a
guarantee of a coherent narrative.

The first accepted insertion is:
\begin{quote}\small
Nora stops Leon. Leon stops Nora. Nora stops Noel. Noel spots Nora.
\end{quote}
with its paired block:
\begin{quote}\small
Aron stops Leon. Leon spots Aron. Aron spots Noel. Noel spots Aron.
\end{quote}
Each block has 52 letters. Their normalized tapes are reversals, so the
combined edit adds 104 letters and produces the exact 672-letter passage.
None of the original 568 letters is removed. The saved run records 9,273
candidate-frontier examinations, 35 rejected-attempt records, and one
accepted chain. These counters describe a deterministic first-success search
on this inventory, not 9,273 independent trials or a measured probability
of readable output. A new, separately implemented replay over the frozen
relation index reproduces the complete text, digest, and all three counters;
the 35 rejected records comprise 27 chain-gate rejections and eight
character-residual rejections. It performs no new historical novelty scan.

The archive index used by this run includes a retrospective snapshot.
A separately recorded scan checks the inserted relation strings against
an earlier commit. We report the resulting archive-relative checks as
provenance, not as a preregistered held-out test. This search demonstrates
the executable mechanism separately from the authored editing recipes.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Independent reconstruction and validation}

Our audit reads rendered strings directly from the selected saved artifacts
and does not import their generators or trust their stored exactness flags.
It independently extracts ASCII letters and compares the tape with its
reversal. A second implementation scans the raw text from both ends,
skipping every character outside ASCII \code{A--Z}/\code{a--z} and comparing
each surviving pair. Both exactness checks must pass. Normalized length and
digest are compared separately with the pinned artifact for every example. SHA-256 identifies
the tape; hash equality is a useful integrity check but is not the proof of
palindromicity. The character comparisons establish exactness.

Source-file digests and a frozen repository snapshot make accidental changes
to the inputs detectable. The audit retains complete text, not only hashes or
an excerpt selected for fluency. Eleven examples pass both exactness checks.
These are eleven selected strings, many sharing an ancestor, so they are not
eleven independent experimental replications.

\subsection{Exact growth and repetition}
\label{sec:results}

The sequence 568 $\rightarrow$ 640 $\rightarrow$ 686 $\rightarrow$ 736
$\rightarrow$ 752 grows by 184 letters. The 640 child adds an observation
chain. The next two edits replace mirrored question/fragment shells with
event chains, first with repeated \emph{sees} predicates and then with
\emph{sees}, \emph{stops}, and \emph{spots}. The final edit replaces a
central phrase pair with ``Ari sees Leon. Leon stops Ira.'' and ``Ari spots
Noel. Noel sees Ira.'' These local revisions are executable and exact; their
left event content was authored and their right paths were parsed from the
opposing tape. The 616 and 630 examples are separate children
of 568, not steps on this sequence. The searched 672 passage is another branch.

We report word counts and repeated sentence segments alongside letter
length. Tokens are ASCII alphabetic strings with optional internal
apostrophes, lowercased for type counts. A sentence segment ends at a
period, question mark, or exclamation mark. Duplicate segments are
counted after lowercasing and whitespace normalization as excess occurrences
of an identical segment, retaining punctuation. The supplementary audit also
records excess repeated three-token
sequences divided by all three-token positions. These reproducible measures
identify repeated material, but they do not measure comprehensibility.
In particular, changing names can eliminate an identical segment while
preserving a repetitive sentence pattern. This distinction is visible in the
568-to-752 sequence: letter length grows by 32.4\%, duplicate sentence
segments fall from three to zero, and vocabulary size falls from 55 to 48
types. Removing exact duplicates does not imply greater lexical variety.

The 752 rendering still contains ``Leon.'' as an isolated fragment and
``Aidan's drawer, Aratos, spots Aidan'' as an opaque clause. It also contains
a proper palindromic central event span. Those observations prevent a claim
that the longest audited endpoint resolves the English-generation objective.
They do not erase its independently verified growth. Appendix~\ref{app:examples}
prints the full passage so that this distinction is inspectable.

The short reference and its 54-letter seed-based extension provide additional
context. The latter reads ``An aide rips nine memo-hero memos. Some more home
men inspire Diana.'' Its exactness is established, while \emph{memo-hero}
and \emph{home men} require interpretation. The inherited 38-letter reference
is neither a mechanical upper bound nor an original result claimed here.

\subsection{Checking the editing invariant}
\label{sec:invariant-check}

A separate exhaustive check enumerates binary strings with $|A|,|q|\leq2$,
palindromic $C$ of length at most four, and $|U|=|V|\leq3$. All 54,145
cases agree with Equation~\ref{eq:seam}; an additional 91 outer/center
checks pass. Empty strings are included as algebraic boundary cases.
The unequal-length counterexample $A=C=U=\epsilon$, $q=V=\code{a}$
yields the palindrome \code{aaa} while violating the equation, illustrating
the scope of the equivalence. This bounded software check complements the
proof; it does not model English.

\section{Language analysis and reader evidence}
\label{sec:readers}

The 736-to-752 edit illustrates the difference between a local language
change and whole-text understanding. It replaces ``Ari delivers maps'' /
``Spam's reviled, Ira'' with two pairs of complete transitive clauses.
``Ari sees Leon. Leon stops Ira.'' specifies actors and actions clearly in
isolation. The matching ``Ari spots Noel. Noel sees Ira.'' also has ordinary
local syntax. Their relationship to the surrounding text still needs an
interpretation; exact matching supplies none.

We distinguish ease of reading, local grammaticality, and discourse coherence,
following the need for explicit evaluation dimensions emphasized by
\citet{howcroft-etal-2020-twenty}. A sentence may be grammatical while its
relation to the next sentence is unclear; likewise, a reader may infer a
meaning from a fragment without finding the passage fluent.

No reader scores appear in this paper. The existing 54-letter packet has
24 prepared forms and no completed responses in the audited evidence.
That form count is not a participant count. Qualitative AI reviews of the
672 construction describe locally grammatical clauses but unresolved
whole-passage readability; these are development feedback, not blinded
human observations.

Appendix~\ref{app:readers} outlines a proposed blinded evaluation with intact
and shuffled controls. It is a plan for measuring these distinctions, not
an additional experimental result.

\section{Related work}

\citet{norvig2002palindrome} describes phrase- and letter-based palindrome
construction with unmatched remainders. Our residual notation follows that
basic construction problem. \citet{papadopoulos2015palindromes} combine
forward and backward corpus graphs to handle characters and word transitions,
and incorporate syntactic patterns. Their method already addresses arbitrary
length within a defined corpus language. We study edits of a supplied
palindrome and preserve the particular retained text across those edits;
we do not claim the first use of syntax during palindrome generation.

Lexically constrained decoding maintains requirements during generation
\citep{hokamp2017grid}. Here the constraint is equality with an opposing
character sequence, whose word boundaries can differ. The searched operator
uses a finite grammar rather than a trained language model, while the
authored recipes expose lexical decisions directly. Human evaluation of
generated language requires defined constructs and reproducible procedures
\citep{howcroft-etal-2020-twenty,belz-etal-2021-reprogen}; mechanical symmetry
alone does not address either.

\section{Conclusion}

Residual-constrained editing provides a reproducible way to modify long
letter-level palindromes while preserving a verified parent. We make the
local spelling equation, retained text, and proposal source explicit, and
independently verify selected constructions through 752 letters. A separate
typed-clause search constructs a 672-letter child. The resulting passages
make progress in exact construction visible, including the repeated language
and unresolved seams that still need revision. The next substantive test is
whether edits improve readers' understanding of the full passages.

\section*{Limitations}

The long examples share an inherited parent and were selected
retrospectively. Their outcomes do not estimate population success rates.
Most left edit proposals are authored; the clause-search example uses a
small, deliberately reverse-compatible vocabulary, fixed depth, and a
first-success stop. It does not establish autonomous generation of arbitrary
English or a scaling law. Proper palindromic spans, repeated lexical material,
and rough prose remain. Archive-relative absence does not prove originality,
and we have not independently established the origin of every inherited motif.
No completed blinded human study, calibrated automatic readability measure,
or comparative language-quality evaluation supports a claim that the long
texts are readable. We have not compared against the corpus-graph baseline
of \citet{papadopoulos2015palindromes}. The ASCII definition excludes accented letters and is
not a multilingual normalization policy.

\section*{Ethical considerations and assistance}

The evaluated artifacts are synthetic text and software traces. The reported
audit contains no newly collected participant data. Any future reader study
requires appropriate consent, compensation, and institutional review under
the authors' applicable procedures. Generative AI assisted the development
of construction recipes, code, manuscript drafting, and internal review.
These internal reviews are not independent human evaluation. The authors
remain responsible for source verification and claims. At submission, this
assistance and its scope must also be declared in the venue's responsible
research checklist and any required acknowledgment.

\section{Prospective blinded reader evaluation}
\label{app:readers}

A reader study should freeze candidate text and include both intact ordinary
prose and token-shuffled controls. Opaque item identifiers and independently
randomized orders conceal construction provenance. Each participant should
see only one version of a given content item, preventing an intact control
from teaching the intended reading of its shuffled counterpart. Besides
separate ordinal questions for fluency and coherence, event-paraphrase
questions can test whether readers agree about who did what. Coding those
answers should remain blind to condition. Sample size, exclusion rules,
assignment, and analysis must be fixed before collecting responses. This
is a proposed evaluation, not a preregistered or completed experiment.
