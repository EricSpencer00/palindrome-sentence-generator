Addendum: Scoring the Branches

An addition to World's Longest Palindrome?, written to be read after it.
program, live

Norvig's search is the search used here. The remainder of steps (2) through (7) above is called the overhang below, and it is the same object: the run of letters one half owes the other, which a word added on the left must match forwards and a word added on the right must match reversed. Hoey's 1984 program is the ancestor of both.

What changes is what the search is for. Norvig's program asks, at each step, which choice will let it keep going — randomly in versions 1 and 2, and in version 3 by the letter that "leads to the most completions of words on both left and right." Both are length. The 21,012-word palindrome is the answer to a question about length, and it is a very good one.

The question here is what the text says. At every step the overhang admits hundreds of letter-valid continuations and nearly all of them are gibberish, so the choice among them is free — it costs no letters, it is made hundreds of times, and nothing in the constraint cares which way it goes. That is where a language model goes. It cannot make a palindrome and it is never asked to: the search guarantees the mirror, and every candidate is valid in every configuration measured below. The model only decides which of the valid branches to follow.

The algorithm. Steps (1), (2) and (6) are Norvig's unchanged. Step (3) is the whole difference:

(3) Look in the dictionary for words that begin with aca. Norvig randomly chooses one. Here, all of them are scored by a language model in the context of what is already written, and the beam keeps the best sixty.
A man, a plan, a caddy, a canal, Panama
(3a) The same step, said as the thing that is actually being spent. The overhang does not care which of the candidates is chosen — every one of them leaves the text a valid palindrome — so the choice is free with respect to the constraint and not free with respect to the reading. There are hundreds of such choices in a thousand-letter palindrome, and taking them at random is what makes a long palindrome unreadable. It is not the constraint that does it.

Two places to put the model, and the cost of each. Twelve seeds, 200 letters minimum, beam 60, GPT-2 small, matched budgets. lm_score is mean token log-probability per letter; higher is better.

In-loop reads measurably better at roughly 31× the cost. Both arms close every seed, which is the point about validity: it comes from the search, so the model can only move readability.

A warning about that number, since it is the number everything here was tuned on. lm_score divides total token log-probability by letters. Longer words cost fewer tokens per letter, so a search that merely prefers long words scores higher without writing anything better. A tuning run found that on its own: +0.298 per letter, winning 24 of 24 paired seeds, while the same texts scored 0.673 worse per token and lost 24 of 24. A check against a bigger model did not catch it, because the check used the same denominator. Every experiment in the repository now prints both normalizations and says so when they disagree.

Commentary: A search reaches its length and stops; you take what it gives you. The other way to get a palindrome of a stated length is to assemble it from parts that already pay the mirror, and the parts that work are mirror-pairs: two different texts whose letters are each other's reverse. Nested around a centre, 

        L1 L2 ... Lk   CENTRE   Rk ... R2 R1
where each Ri is Li's letters reversed. The whole mirrors by construction and nothing repeats.

The obvious alternative — stringing together palindromes, each palindromic on its own — does not work, and the reason is worth writing down. A sequence of self-palindromic units is a palindrome only when the sequence is itself a palindrome, so unit k must equal unit n+1−k and every unit but the centre appears twice. Mirror-pairs avoid it: what returns at the mirrored position is the other half, which is different text.

The pairs come from an offline walk of millions of candidates — 540 verified palindromes, 499 of them found by this project's own enumeration and 41 from the record. So length becomes a parameter rather than an outcome, up to the roughly 14,500 letters of material the bank holds. It is a dial on the page.

Punctuation is free and is chosen rather than guessed. Case, spaces and marks are invisible to the mirror — the same licence taken by "A man, a plan, a canal: Panama" — so every way of cutting the word run into runs is scored, each run by the strongest Brown-tag test it passes, and the best total wins. reno sir parasites set i sara prisoner is written out as 

        Reno, sir: parasites set. I, Sara, prisoner.
The letters are asserted unchanged before the result is returned. Presentation that alters the palindrome is a bug, not a style.

The Comparison

Not length. Norvig's version 3 is 90,439 letters and nothing here is within an order of magnitude of that; that is the difference between the two questions, not a result about either. What is comparable is the material: what fraction of the words on the page are words a reader accepts. Just under half of the words in the 21,012-word palindrome are not words in that sense — 47.6% of them. That is not a flaw in it. It is where the length comes from: PETN, ILGWU, Roydd, Aeniah and 126,342 entries of Moby are exactly the reversible material a search needs, and Norvig says so himself — "there are 126,000 words in the dictionary, but only about 10% of them are easily reversible." Every proper noun and abbreviation ruled out here is length given up on purpose.

And the column that does not flatter this method is the last one. Mean word length is 3.09 letters for the search and about 3.5 for the composition, against 4.39 for English and 4.27 for Norvig's palindrome. His text uses words of roughly English length and they are mostly not English words; this one uses real words that are mostly short. Both are ways of being unlike English prose, and the second is not obviously the better one. The one- and two-letter share says the same thing: 22.9% for Norvig against 21.7% for English, which is to say his is normal, and version 1's 32.7% is not.

Speed: The deployed search closes 1,010 letters in about 16 seconds and will run to any length asked of it; it does not read better at any of them. The composition answers in well under a second at any length, because the walk that found the material happened offline and took minutes on 32 cores.

What This Does Not Claim

That the text is grammatical, that it has a subject a reader can name, or that it reads as prose rather than as sentences that happen to parse. Those need blind judging against salad and real-prose controls. Four automated proxies have been tried in this project and none has ever agreed with blind ranking, so they are not offered here as evidence.

Two things that were measured and came out against the method are recorded with the rest, because leaving them out is how the number above would become untrue:

  • Growing a palindrome by wrapping it in mirrored words works mechanically — 750 of 750 seeds grew, mean +36 letters — and two blind annotators, position balanced and in complete agreement, then preferred the ungrown seed on 20 of 20 pairs. Growth is a length hack. It is off by default.
  • Assembly guarantees that no chunk repeats, and that guarantee holds. It does not guarantee that no sentence repeats: punctuation is applied to the assembled run rather than per chunk, so two unrelated chunks holding the same short word run get cut into the same sentence. Past about 1,200 letters that happens nearly always.
